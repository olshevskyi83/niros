18 Abstract


19


20 Classic psychedelics alter sense of self and patterns of self-related thought. These changes are


21 hypothesised to underlie their therapeutic efficacy across internalising pathologies such as addiction,


22 anxiety, and depression. Using resting-state functional MRI images from a randomised, double blinded,


23 placebo-controlled clinical trial of 24 healthy adults under 0.215mg/kg psilocybin, we investigated the


24 effective connectivity changes between the amygdala and the default mode network (DMN), salience


25 network (SN) and central executive network (CEN). This connectivity underpins the appraisal and


26 regulation of emotion and is associated with clinical symptomatology. We observed a general pattern of


27 decreased top-down effective connectivity from the resting state networks of interest to the amygdala and


28 directed connectivity changes associated with altered emotion and meaning under psilocybin. Our

35 Introduction


36


37 Psilocybin, the main active compound of so-called magic mushrooms, is a serotonergic psychedelic that


38 has been used throughout history in religious and medical rituals to alter consciousness (Carod-Artal,


39 2015; Nichols, 2016). It belongs to a larger group of substances such as ayahuasca (DMT) and mescaline


40 that share molecular binding kinetics to serotonergic 5-HT receptors distributed throughout the brain


41 (Nichols, 2012). Agonism of the 5-HT2A receptor is primarily responsible for psychedelic subjective


42 effects that can elicit powerful changes to subjective experience (Vollenweider et al 1998, Kometer et al.


43 2013, Preller et al. 2017, Kraehenmann et al. 2017). These include profound alterations in self-


44 consciousness and perception including visual illusions, hallucinations, and synaesthesia, emotion and


45 cognitive capacities, and transcendence of space and time (Studerus, Gamma, & Vollenweider, 2010;


46 Swanson, 2018; Wittmann et al., 2007; Stoliker, Egan, Friston, & Razi, 2021). Serotonergic psychedelic-


47 induced alterations can also involve an experience of ego dissolution defined as a loss of self-boundaries


48 and sense of unity between the self and the environment (Stoliker, et al., 2021; Lebedev et al., 2015).


49 Although, complete ego dissolution is rare at the doses used in clinical trials, several studies have


50 associated measurable degrees of ego dissolution (indexed as total mystical score or oceanic self-


51 boundlessness) to symptom reduction of depression (Griffiths et al., 2016; Roseman, Nutt, & Carhart-


52 Harris, 2018; Ross et al., 2016) and addiction (Bogenschutz et al., 2015; Garcia-Romeu, Griffiths, &


53 Johnson, 2014). However, the cognitive and emotional neural mechanisms that underlie psychedelic


54 subjective and therapeutic effects are not well understood. Therefore, our goal was to investigate

57


58 Previous research has demonstrated change to the amygdala and associative connectivity under


59 psychedelics. The amygdala’s role in emotional arousal and the function of associative connectivity in


60 cognition identify the amygdala connectivity as a target to investigate emotional and cognitive change


61 under psilocybin. The amygdala is a bilateral subcortical region composed of many subnuclei that


62 integrate cortical and thalamic sensory inputs and their emotional valence (Babaev, Piletti Chatain, &


63 Krueger-Burg, 2018). For example, presentations of positive or negative emotional stimuli—such as


64 physical threats—elicit activation of the amygdala (Alexander et al., 2021; Garavan, Pendergrass, Ross,


65 Stein, & Risinger, 2001; Hobin, Goosens, & Maren, 2003; Weymar & Schwabe, 2016). The amygdala is


66 also well known for its involvement in fear conditioning (Sun, Gooch, & Sah, 2020) and activates in


67 experiences of emotional empathy (Geng et al., 2018; Hillis, 2014; Marsh et al., 2013). Hyperactivation


68 of the amygdala is a marker of stress and anxiety and is implicated across internalising disorders for


69 which psychedelics demonstrate efficacy (Nutt & Carhart-Harris, 2020; Koob & Volkow, 2010; Luo,


70 Xue, Shen, & Lu, 2013; Menon, 2011). Interestingly, the amygdala also responds to beliefs (Kaplan,


71 Gimbel, & Harris, 2016) suggesting that the amygdala conditions an emotional response to self-related


72 beliefs that may underline internalising disorders. Damage to the amygdala affects decision-making


73 (Gupta, Koscik, Bechara, & Tranel, 2011), memory (Adolphs, Tranel, & Denburg, 2000), and behaviour


74 regulation. Moreover, its damage can also diminish an individual's sense of shame (Piretti et al., 2020)


75 further suggesting the amygdala mediates the relationship between emotion and cognition.


76


77 The amygdala is integrated with cognition through top-down projections from midline cortical regions


78 (Etkin et al., 2011; Kraehenmann et al., 2016; Robinson et al., 2013) that underlie the perception and


79 intensity of positive and negative emotions (Bonnet et al., 2015). This circuitry also underlies


80 psychopathological conditions such as anxiety disorders (Menon, 2011). Modulation of top-down control

81 by psilocybin suggests influence on limbic region emotional processing (Bernasconi et al., 2014). For


82 example, healthy adults administered psilocybin show reduced effective amygdala connectivity to the


83 primary visual cortex, coinciding with reduced threat processing (Kraehenmann et al., 2016). The


84 semisynthetic psychedelic drug lysergic diethylamide acid (LSD) also has been shown to dampen the


85 amygdala response to the presentation of fearful faces (Mueller et al., 2017). Moreover, decreased


86 amygdala activity has been correlated with positive mood under psilocybin (Kraehenmann et al., 2015).


87 Together, these results suggest a decrease in amygdala connectivity in association with therapeutic


88 response in healthy adults under the acute influence of psychedelics.


89


90 In contrast, clinical populations measured the day after administration of psilocybin demonstrated an


91 opposite pattern of amygdala activity. The amygdala response to emotional faces has been reported to


92 increase the day after psilocybin administration (Roseman, Demetriou, Wall, Nutt, & Carhart-Harris,


93 2018) and be accompanied by decreased functional connectivity with the prefrontal cortex (Mertens et al.,


94 2020; Mueller et al., 2017). It has been proposed that increased amygdala activity in the sub-acute period


95 may underlie a sense of emotional reconnection (Watts, Day, Krzanowski, Nutt, & Carhart-Harris, 2017).


96 Changes to the amygdala and cortical regions are sustained well beyond the half-life of psilocybin and


97 may last up to one month post administration (Barrett, Doss, Sepeda, Pekar, & Griffiths, 2020),


98 demonstrating the lasting neural changes in association with positive responses to emotionally conflicting


99 stimuli. These results indicate the acute effects of psychedelics in clinical studies may disrupt conditioned


100 response patterns and produce enduring connectivity changes that support therapeutic modes of


101 behaviour.


102


103 The importance of emotion and cognition to behaviour motivate investigation of cortical regions with


104 established connections and associations to the amygdala. These include frontal and anterior regions that


105 form nodes of distinct large-scale resting-state networks (RSNs). RSNs are determined by temporally


106 correlated activation across spatially distributed brain regions and serve essential functions in cognition

107 (Fox et al., 2005; Raichle, 2015). The RSN regions of particular relevance to this study are abundant in 5-


108 HT2A receptors (R. L. Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). The functional integration of RSN nodes with the


109 amygdala illustrate that emotion is a flexible and dynamic workspace within the brain (Lindquist, Satpute,


110 Wager, Weber, & Barrett, 2016).


111


112 The default mode network (DMN) integrates internal and external information to perform self-referential


113 mental activity that is closely identified with the sense of self. DMN hub regions comprise the medial


114 prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC). The mPFC functions in temporally


115 extended goal-directed thinking and regulation of behaviour, while the PCC orients information towards


116 the self (Andrews-Hanna, Smallwood, & Spreng, 2014; J. A. Brewer, Garrison, & Whitfield-Gabrieli,


117 2013). Information integration in the DMN sustains self-evaluative processes involved in forming self-


118 beliefs (Amey, Leitner, Liu, & Forbes, 2022; Tompson, Chua, & Kitayama, 2016).


119


120 DMN connectivity is related to wellbeing. For example, limbic-cortical dysregulation have been reported


121 to impact mood regulation (Zeng et al., 2012) and increased FC between the hippocampus and prefrontal


122 cortex (e.g., mPFC) was observed in depression (Kaiser, Andrews-Hanna, Wager, & Pizzagalli, 2015).


123 Increased DMN connectivity with the amygdala is also observed in depressed subjects (Sheline Yvette et


124 al., 2009) in association with symptoms such as rumination (Cooney, Joormann, Eugéne, Dennis, &


125 Gotlib, 2010). In contrast, decreased PCC-mPFC coupling is associated with the reduced mind wandering


126 of advanced meditation practitioners (Judson A. Brewer et al., 2011) with similar decoupling found


127 acutely (Carhart -Harris et al 2012) and the day after psilocybin (R. L.; Smigielski, Scheidegger, Kometer,


128 & Vollenweider, 2019).


129


130 DMN frontal regions, specifically the mPFC, share well-established connectivity to the amygdala that


131 plays a role in the valence and regulation of emotion (Willinger et al., 2019; Underwood, Tolmeijer,


132 Wibroe, Peters, & Mason, 2021; Cooney et al., 2010). The PCC node of the DMN is less often associated

133 with the amygdala. However, it plays a significant role in the experience of self. Under psychedelics,


134 reduced PCC connectivity is associated with diminished feelings of self that align with descriptions of


135 ego dissolution (R. L. Carhart-Harris et al., 2014) and reduced self-representation in social interaction


136 (Preller et al., 2018). We hypothesise reduced interactions between the amygdala and DMN and reduced


137 within-DMN connectivity may underwrite altered sense of self and self-related emotion under


138 psychedelic subjective effects and serve as a biomarker of connectivity. that may be therapeutic.


139


140 Frontal regions also compose the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dIPFC) nodes of the central executive


141 network (CEN). The CEN is a RSN involved in thinking, planning, and controlling attention (Andrews-


142 Hanna et al., 2014; Menon, 2011). The dIPFC serves a primary role in regulating emotion (Underwood et


143 al., 2021) and handles content in working memory and evaluates possible behavioural responses. The


144 CEN also includes the lateral posterior parietal cortex (IPPC), which integrates sensory and interoceptive


145 information to facilitate sustained attention (Borders, 2020). CEN functions are associated with mental


146 health in various cognitive disorders. Hyper and hypo-frontal connectivity are often markers of disordered


147 cognition and anxious behaviour. For example, increased CEN-AMG connectivity was observed in


148 association with the catastrophising of chronic pain (Jiang et al., 2016), and dIPFC connectivity was


149 associated with worry in healthy adults and individuals with generalised anxiety (Paulesu et al., 2010;


150 Servaas et al., 2014; Jamieson, Harrison, Razi, & Davey, 2022). Moreover, reduced CEN activity has


151 been associated with chronic schizophrenia and depression (Leptourgos et al., 2020; Menon, 2011).


152 However, links between CEN activity and behaviour are suggested to be task and context-dependent


153 (Borders, 2020). For example, reduced dIPFC was associated with reduced rumination observed in


154 healthy adults (Cooney et al., 2010).


155

157 functional pathways and proliferates to regions outside of this network. For example, LSD increases

162 changes with the amygdala, such as the CEN, under psychedelics have not been well explored and may


163 underlie change to cognitive-emotional regulation.


164


165 The salience network (SN) is a cognitive RSN involved in detecting stimuli associated with biological or


166 goal-directed personal significance. Both the DMN and CEN are under the influence of the SN (Menon &


167 Uddin, 2010; Sridharan, Levitin, & Menon, 2008). The SN recruits CEN activity to provide task-relevant


168 information in response to detection of salient features in the environment and disengages task-irrelevant


169 activity, for example, daydreaming. The SN's cardinal regions are the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex


170 (dACC) and anterior insula (AI). The dACC is involved in response selection and conflict monitoring


171 (Ide, Shenoy, Yu, & Li, 2013; Menon, 2011) and activated in response to social pain (Rotge et al., 2015).


172 The AI detects behaviourally relevant stimuli (Menon, 2015) and receives multimodal sensory input


173 (Averbeck & Seo, 2008; Vogt & Pandya, 1987). Expanded network compositions of the SN also include


174 the amygdala, which may be important for the initial emotional appraisal of salient stimuli (Menon,


175 2011). Overactive salience attributed to stimuli is a feature of various forms of psychopathology.


176 Hyperactivity of the AI or amygdala has been reported to underwrite enhanced salience detection


177 (Menon, 2011). Moreover, increased salience and attention to the self during social interactions may


178 underlie social anxiety, while overt salience and attention to negative stimuli or mentation has been


179 suggested to reinforce depressed thinking. The salience of channels of attention (1.e., the top-down


180 selection of bottom-up information that receives priority in attention) also plays a role in psychosis,


181 suggesting the SN may act as pre-reflective mechanisms of attention that underlie perception and beliefs


182 (Kapur, 2003).

184 Serotonergic receptors play a role in the selection of channels of attention (Stoliker, et al., 2021), and


185 observations under psilocybin have shown reduced SN integrity (Lebedev et al., 2015). Changes to


186 effective connectivity from the SN to the DMN are also observed under LSD (Stoliker, Novelli, et al.,


187 2021). The dACC has been associated with changes in self-processing and a reduced sense of social


188 exclusion under psilocybin administration that did not depend on changes in attention and awareness


189 (Preller et al., 2016; Preller et al., 2015). However, amygdala links with the dACC and AI under


190 psychedelics remain relatively unexplored and worthy of investigation for their contribution to the


191 subjective effects and therapeutic benefits of psilocybin.


192


193 To address the gap in understanding emotion and cognition changes under psychedelics, we apply


194 spectral dynamic causal modelling (spDCM) (Karl J. Friston, Kahan, Biswal, & Razi, 2014; Razi, Kahan,


195 Rees, & Friston, 2015; Razi et al., 2017). DCM can infer the direction of connectivity between


196 hierarchically organised brain regions composing RSNs (Zhou et al., 2018) and DCM has been used to


197 measure the effective connectivity under LSD from the thalamus to the cortex (Preller et al., 2019) and


198 among various RSNs (DMN, SN, DAN) (Stoliker, Novelli, et al., 2021). The ability of DCM to


199 investigate bottom-up and top-down processes between regions of the brain suggests it as a valuable


200 technique to investigate psilocybin-induced effective connectivity changes between high-level RSNs and


201 the subcortical amygdala. Moreover, DCM allows the inference of association between effective


202 connectivity changes and retrospective behavioural reports using the Bayesian framework of parametric


203 empirical Bayes (PEB) (K. J. Friston et al., 2016). We measured changed meaning of


204 precepts and blissful state collected 360 min following the administration of psilocybin using the five


205 dimensions of altered states of consciousness scale (SD-ASC) (Studerus et al., 2010).


206

208 associative RSNs we examined express high density of the 5-HT2AR that are thought to alter the top-


209 down modulation of subcortical connectivity. Previous reports also suggest psilocybin can induce

289 Discussion:


290


291 To investigate the influence of psilocybin on the interaction between emotion and cognition, we measured


292 connectivity changes between the amygdala and selected RSNs. These networks serve functions in


293 — selfhood and cognition and are composed of regions linked to the regulation and experience of emotion.


294 Our results demonstrate increased inhibitory effective connectivity from cortical regions to the amygdala


295 under psilocybin. For example, we showed this change in connectivity from the PCC, IdPFC, bilateral


296 IPPC, ACC, and IAI. These results suggest cognitive resources do not recruit the amygdala under


297 — psilocybin, and this may relate to subjective and therapeutic effects. of psilocybin. While we did not find


298 effective connectivity change involving the mPFC—which has been frequently cited in association with


299 emotion, such as emotional valence (Willinger et al., 2019)—examination of the PCC and amygdala


300 revealed multiple connectivity changes and behavioural associations under psilocybin. PCC and amygdala


301 effective connectivity change was bidirectional and accompanied by reduced PCC self-inhibition, which


302 indicates increased synaptic gain or its sensitivity to inputs. Increased PCC synaptic gain was associated


303 with the subjective experience of blissful state, and PCC bilateral inhibition with the left amygdala was


304 associated with changed meaning of precepts. Our findings suggest inhibition between amygdala-


305 mediated emotion and self-referential functions as an important factor in these subjective changes that


306 may contribute to the joyous feelings of self-dissolution of ego dissolution and reduced self-other

309


310 IDLPFC and bilateral ILPPC inhibition to the amygdala is situated within the CEN. Connectivity in this


311 network is believed to underlie the evaluative aspect of thought, with the DLPFC playing a central role in


312 emotional regulation. However, IDLPFC inhibition to the amygdala was not associated with subjective


313 effects. Instead, the pattern of activity in this network is open to interpretation. One characterisation of the


314 observed activity may be its resemblance to hyper-frontality, which is associated with worry and anxiety


315 (Park, Wood, Bondi, Del Arco, & Moghaddam, 2016), and acute psychosis (Vollenweider et al., 1997).


316 — Participants in this study were experiencing the effects of psychedelics in the setting of an fMRI scan,


317 which may arouse substantial anxiety. Moreover, strong parallels between cortical activity patterns


318 associated with psychedelic subjective effects have been shown to resemble anxiety (Stoliker, et al.,


319 2021). However, subjects of this study reported nominal anxiety, see Supplementary Material. An


320 alternative interpretation is that the observed connectivity pattern between regions of the CEN constitutes


321 an increase in evaluative thought processes. This interpretation aligns with the function of the CEN and

418 In sum, we demonstrated altered within-RSN effective connectivity and reduced top-down RSN-

422 subjective wellbeing. Psilocybin altered cognition underwritten by connectivity changes may contribute to


423 lasting therapeutic outcomes and enable the emotional and cognitive flexibility that facilitate alternate


424 selection of perceptual hypotheses which underwrite self-beliefs. In particular, we suggest RSN


425 attenuation of the amygdala as a potential biomarker for future clinical research investigating the neural


426 correlates of psychedelic therapeutic efficacy. Future work may also build upon our directed connectivity


427 findings to understand how connectivity involved in emotion and cognition interact with the therapeutic


428 context to effect patterns of thought and behaviour. These lines of enquiry can help discern the imperative


429 of the psychedelic subjective experience to therapeutic outcomes and bridge gaps in our understanding of


430 the relationship between brain connectivity and behaviour.

525 and the covariance of the parameters were taken into account. That is, precise parameter estimates
